Europe, China? ............................................................. <i>Silurotaenia</i> Nybelin, 1942</p><p>SPECIES COMPOSITION OF <i>GANGESIA</i></p><p>Ash <i>et al.</i> (2015) synonymized numerous poorly described, insufficiently differentiated species of <i>Gangesia</i> from the Indomalayan region. Since then, Jasrotia &amp; Kaur (2017) described <i>G</i>. <i>punjabensis</i> Jasrotia &amp; Kaur, 2017 from <i>Wallago attu</i> in India based on alleged molecular support (GenBank accession number KY018599). However, the sequence KY018599 of Jasrotia &amp; Kaur (2017) includes obvious errors, because it includes the primer regions (containing errors) used by the authors for PCR amplification and sequencing. This error questioned the quality of the sequence. Moreover, if we exclude the primer regions from KY018599, there are only two single cytosine insertions left, both situated close to the 5\u02b9 and 3\u02b9 ends of the sequence, i.e. positions of likely lower sequencing quality. Otherwise, the KY018599 <i>lsr</i> DNA fragment is identical with the remaining four representatives of <i>Gangesia bengalensis</i> (from an identical host species) in the GenBank database. In its morphology, <i>G. punjabensis</i> shares all taxonomically important characters with <i>G. bengalensis</i>, including rostellar hook number (47&ndash;54), number of rows of rostellar hooks (two), number of rows of hooklets on sucker (five to eight), relative size of the cirrus-sac (about one-quarter of width of proglottids) (see: Ash <i>et al.</i>, 2012: figs 1A&ndash;E, 2 and Jasrotia &amp; Kaur, 2017: figs 1, 2A&ndash;B, 3). Therefore, we consider <i>G. punjabensis</i> a new synonym of <i>G. bengalensis</i>.</p><p>Later on, Jasrotia <i>et al.</i> (2019) described another new species of <i>Gangesia</i>, namely <i>Gangesia harikeensis</i> Jasrotia <i>et al.</i>, 2019, based on two specimens collected from the same fish host, <i>Wallago attu</i> Bloch &amp; Schneider, 1801, in Punjab, India with molecular data (GenBank accession no. MK127923). As in the case of <i>G. punjabensis</i>, their molecular analysis is questionable; the node at which the species is placed has no support value and the analysis also placed it as a sister-taxon to the clade consisting of all other <i>Gangesia</i> species. We have also found discrepancies between the data provided in the text and those taken from illustrations (Jasrotia <i>et al.</i>, 2019: figs 1, 2), such as measurements of the width of the rostellar-like organ, sucker width and length of the blade of rostellar hooks. Although the specimens of <i>G. harikeensis</i> were strongly contracted and deformed (see Jasrotia <i>et al.</i>, 2019: fig. 2), the important taxonomic characters, such as the length of the scolex, presence of double rows of rostellar hooks, number of testes, relative size of cirrus-sac and the host species, make it indistinguishable from <i>G. bengalensis</i>. Therefore, <i>G</i>. <i>harikeensis</i> is also synonymized with <i>G</i>. <i>bengalensis</i>.</p><p>Specimens of <i>Gangesia</i> sp. collected by M. Oros and T. Scholz from <i>Silurus</i> cf. <i>soldato\u01b2i</i> Nikolskii &amp; Soin in China (Ash <i>et al.</i>, 2015) in 2009 and 2015, provided to the present authors (MHNG-PLAT 67056), form a distinct group based on <i>COI</i> and concatenated data (Figs 1, 2; Supporting Information, Fig. S1) and probably represent a distinct and undescribed species of <i>Gangesia</i>. Unfortunately, these specimens do not have mature and gravid proglottids, which are necessary for formal description of a new species.</p><p>As stated previously, the new species <i>G. mukutmanipurensis</i> is unique among species of <i>Gangesia</i> in lacking hooks on the rostellum-like organ and hooklets on the anterior rim of the suckers. This peculiarity of its scolex morphology is arguably a case of secondary loss of structures. These reversals appear to be rare among cestodes but have been documented in at least two genera of cyclophyllidean cestodes, namely <i>Taenia</i> Linnaeus, 1758 and <i>Hymenolepis</i> Weinland, 1858, where rostellar hooks are absent in the adults of some species (&Scaron;lais, 1973). The presence of different rostellar types (armed rostellum, rudimentary unarmed rostellum) or the lack of a rostellum in multispecies lineages of Hymenolepididae confirm that rostellar morphology is not a reliable indicator of relationships or taxonomy (Haukisalmi <i>et al.</i>, 2010; Neov <i>et al.</i>, 2019). Neov <i>et al.</i> (2019) showed that partial or entire reduction of the rostellar apparatus is an apomorphic condition that arose separately in all lineages of hymenolepidids. Similarly, multigene analyses of the genus <i>Taenia</i> have demonstrated that the absence of rostellar hooks in <i>T</i>. <i>saginata</i> Goeze, 1782 and <i>T</i>. <i>asiatica</i> Eom &amp; Rim, 1993 is a case of secondary loss (Knapp <i>et al.</i>, 2011; Nakao <i>et al.</i>, 2013). Host associations in different biogeographical regions may play a crucial role for such evolutionary processes (Hoberg, 2006).</p><p>Life-history strategies of parasites are often specific to a particular phase/stage of their life cycle (Kuris &amp; Lafferty, 2000; Poulin, 2011). Interaction of larval helminths with their intermediate hosts can be markedly different from that of adult worms and their definitive hosts (Poulin, 2011). Little is known about the life cycle of species of Gangesia, except for two experimental studies of Demshin (1985) and Shimazu (1999) that confirmed planktonic copepods and prey fish as the first and second intermediate (or paratenic) hosts, respectively. Procercoids are formed in copepods, which are ingested by prey fish and grow to plerocercoids, which are infective to the final (definitive) hosts in which the worms mature. In <i>Gangesia parasiluri</i>, hooklets appear on the rostellum-like organ in procercoids and are also present in plerocercoids, but then disappear and are replaced by newly formed hooks in adults (Shimazu, 1999).</p><p>It is generally accepted that the scolex, being the attachment organ, is often adapted to the particular morphology and physiology of the host intestine, and that &lsquo;mucosal topography&rsquo; is a &lsquo;critical resource&rsquo; for enteric helminths (Hayunga, 1991). It is also presumed that highly host-specific helminths are especially adapted to the architecture of their preferred intestinal sites (Hayunga, 1991). Evidence from certain Tetraphyllidea-like cestodes &ndash; the group from which proteocephalids may have evolved &ndash; suggests that compatibility between scolex morphology and attachment site, along with gut physiology, may determine host specificity (Williams, 1960, 1966, 1968a, b; Randhawa &amp; Burt, 2008). In addition, features of the strobila, specifically the shape of the proglottids, may also closely match the intestinal architecture (Joy <i>et al.</i>, 2009). The evolutionary loss of hooks from the scolex of the new species cannot be readily explained at this time, but it is reasonable to propose that the uniqueness of the scolex in this new taxon may be an adaptive evolutionary change related to the internal gut morphology and physiology of its fish host, <i>Ompok bimaculatus</i>.</p><p>ORIGINS AND HISTORICAL BIOGEOGRAPHY OF EARLY BRANCHING PROTEOCEPHALID LINEAGES (&lsquo; GANGESIINAE&rsquo; <i>SENSU LATO</i>) IN THEIR CATFISH HOSTS</p><p>Previous studies (Ash <i>et al</i>., 2012, 2015) have clarified the taxonomy, host associations and contemporary distribution of several early branching lineages of proteocephalid tapeworms conventionally placed in Gangesiinae, a group of tapeworms occupying the freshwaters of the Indomalayan and contiguous Palaearctic biogeographical regions (Fig. 7). The hosts of species of <i>Gangesia</i> include mainly catfishes of the families Siluridae and Bagridae, with one species in a schilbeid catfish (<i>Eutropichthys \u01b2acha</i> Hamilton, 1822: Schilbeidae). The sisterlineages of <i>Gangesia</i> are <i>Silurotaenia</i>, a parasite of a silurid catfish in the Palaearctic region, and <i>Electrotaenia</i>, a parasite of <i>Malapterurus electricus</i> (Gmelin, 1789) (Malapteruridae) in North Africa (Fig. 7). Representatives of the Acanthotaeniinae, parasites of varanid lizards and snakes, together with several lineages of the Gangesiinae <i>s.l.</i>, form the basal groups of proteocephalids. Three monotypic genera are currently recognized in Gangesiinae, all narrowly specific for their siluroid hosts in the Indomalayan region, and whose phylogenetic relationships to the remaining basal groups remain unclear. <i>Ritacestus ritaii</i> (Verma, 1926) occurs in India as a parasite of <i>Rita rita</i> (Hamilton, 1822), a catfish conventionally placed in Bagridae, but found to be deeply separated from the bagrid clade (Kappas <i>et al.</i>, 2016). <i>Vermaia pseudotropii</i> (Verma, 1928) is a parasite of the schilbeid catfish, <i>Clupisoma garua</i> (Hamilton, 1822) in India. <i>Pangasiocestu</i> s is associated with the pangasiid catfish, <i>Pangasius larnaudii</i> Bocourt, 1866, in Cambodia. In addition, species of <i>Postgangesia</i> are found in <i>Silurus</i> spp. in the Palaearctic region. The phylogeny, host associations and contemporary distribution provide the basis for analysing the historical biogeography of these tapeworm lineages in their catfish hosts, although we are currently handicapped by the poor resolution of relationships among the early diverging clades (<i>Pangasiocestus</i>, <i>Ritacestus</i>, <i>Postgangesia</i> and <i>Vermaia</i>).</p><p>Despite the poor resolution of the phylogenetic relationships among groups occupying the base of the proteocephalid tree, their widespread and largely exclusive association with catfishes (Actinopterygii: Siluriformes) suggest that a discussion of the historical biogeography of catfishes, particularly the siluroids, which host all of the known basal lineages in question (conventional Gangesiinae), may provide insight into the origins of these host&ndash;parasite associations.</p><p>Using phylogenetic and biogeographical analyses based on five nuclear genes and two separate timecalibration methods based on the fossil evidence, Chen <i>et al.</i> (2013) generated chronograms that placed siluriform origins, respectively, at 97 Mya or 117 Mya and the origin of siluroids at 79 Mya or 86 Mya. The chronogram from a molecular clock analysis by Kappas <i>et al.</i> (2016) places the origin of the catfishes (order Siluriformes) in the early Cretaceous (133 Mya) and the divergence of the well-supported Siluroidei at 97 Mya. Both studies support the hypothesis of Gondwanan siluriform origins approximately when Africa and South America were completing their final separation (Fig. 8). The analysis of Dai <i>et al.</i> (2018) has the siluriforms originating at 86&ndash;74 Mya, close in age to the oldest fossil records of catfishes in South America. More importantly for our study, these calibrated reconstructions suggest that siluroids originated at least 43&ndash;24 Mya after the final separation of Gondwana and Laurasia (~140 Mya) and shortly after, or approximately with, the separation of Africa and South America (~110&ndash;100 Mya; Fig. 8). This also means that Siluroidei diverged after India separated from both Africa and Australia and began its long journey in isolation (Smith <i>et al.</i>, 1994; Scotese &amp; Wright, 2018), before finally docking on to the Eurasian landmass in the Eocene, between 50 and 45 Mya (Scotese, 2016; Scotese &amp; Wright, 2018). Meanwhile Siluroidei diversified into the &lsquo;Big Asia&rsquo; and &lsquo;Big Africa&rsquo; clades in Africa and Laurasia during the Late Cretaceous (Kappas <i>et al.</i>, 2016), with a remarkable radiation in the Cenozoic (Diogo, 2004; Kappas <i>et al.</i>, 2016). The presence of Late Cretaceous (Maastrichtian) freshwater siluriform fossils in India (Cione &amp; Prasad, 2002) &ndash; also the oldest catfish fossil in Asia &ndash; indicates that India did have freshwater catfishes during its long isolation, but the limited and fragmentary nature of the fossils do not allow a robust analysis of its affinities with other siluriforms.</p><p>When considered together, the phylogeny and host associations of gangesiine tapeworms and the evolutionary history of siluroids suggest one or more possible scenarios for the origins of their associations. The deepest node in the phylogeny of Proteocephalidae is a polytomy comprising several branches of Gangesiinae (<i>s.l.</i>) along with proteocephalids (Acanthotaeniinae) in varanid lizards and snakes. The oldest possible host association in Gangesiinae would have been that of <i>Ritacestus ritaii</i> with <i>Rita rita</i>, a catfish belonging to an early branching (~97 Myr) lineage of siluroids (but see discussion in the next section). The other associations are probably much younger. The host of <i>Vermaia</i>, <i>Clupisoma garua</i>, is part of the large Afro-Asian catfish family Schilbeidae, estimated to be 50&ndash;55 Myr old. Even allowing for the wider distribution of <i>Pangasiocestus</i> in Pangasiidae Bleeker, 1858, this association is also not older than ~40&ndash;45 Myr, the age of Pangasiidae (and of <i>P. larnaudii</i>, the host of the monotypic <i>Pangasiocestus romani</i> Scholz &amp; de Chambrier, 2012). <i>Postgangesia</i> spp. are parasites of <i>Silurus</i> catfishes, a relatively recently evolved lineage (~23 Myr, Kappas <i>et al.</i>, 2016). The remaining gangesiines (<i>Electrotaenia</i> + <i>Silurotaenia</i> + <i>Gangesi a</i>) form a clade that is also part of this unresolved basal polytomy. <i>Malapterurus electricus</i>, the host of <i>Electrotaenia</i>, is an old lineage of catfishes (divergence at ~60 Mya). Therefore, several lineages of hosts were already present in Eurasia when India finally merged with it (Fig. 9).</p><p>The evolution of the remaining gangesiines, i.e. species of <i>Gangesia</i>, involves radiation in catfish hosts, including several silurids (<i>Wallago attu</i>, <i>Ompok bimaculatus</i> and <i>Silurus asotus</i>) and bagrids (<i>Tachysurus ful\u01b2idraco, Sperata seenghala</i> and <i>Mystus</i> spp.). Bagridae evolved ~62 Mya, with <i>Tachysurus ful\u01b2idraco</i> (syn. <i>Pelteobagrus ful\u01b2idraco</i>) originating around the same time (~22 Mya) as <i>Silurus</i>. The age of other bagrid hosts of <i>Gangesia</i>, such as <i>S. seenghala</i> (the host of <i>G. macrones</i> in India), remains unknown but, given the position of <i>G. macrones</i> on the tree, we can infer that it too was part of a Neogene radiation of these tapeworms. The origin of the hookless <i>Gangesia mukutmanipurensis</i> in the silurid <i>Ompok bimaculatus</i> is also firmly embedded in the radiation of <i>Gangesia</i> in catfishes of the Indomalayan region during this geological period. Whether Gangesiinae evolved early in the evolutionary history of siluroid catfishes will depend on the position of lineages that are associated with evolutionarily older siluroid lineages, such as <i>Rita</i>, <i>Malapterurus</i> and <i>Pangasius</i>, relative to those in younger catfish clades, such as <i>Silurus</i>.</p><p>AN &lsquo; OUT OF INDOMALAYA&rsquo; OR AN &lsquo; INTO INDOMALAYA&rsquo; HYPOTHESIS FOR GANGESIINAE?</p><p>The current polytomy of several gangesiine lineages at the base of the phylogenetic tree limits our reconstructions of the historical biogeography (assuming that this is not a &lsquo;hard&rsquo; polytomy) of these tapeworms. Nevertheless, several of these deep lineages of Gangesiinae (<i>Pangasiocestus</i> and <i>Ritacestus</i>) are associated with the Indomalayan region, and the diversification of Gangesiinae (e.g. <i>Gangesia</i>) also involves this region. The siluroid <i>Pangasius</i>, host of <i>Pangasiocestus</i>, is endemic to South and South-East Asia (the Indomalayan region). <i>Rita rita</i>, a species of an early branching siluroid lineage (and host of <i>Ritacestus</i>), is widely distributed across the Indian subcontinent and Burma. All other species of <i>Rita</i> are also restricted to South Asia. Despite its old evolutionary age, <i>Rita</i> probably did not evolve by drift vicariance when India separated from Africa because it is not consistent with the geological history of India. By the time India became contiguous with Eurasia, the predominant siluroid host groups had already originated and diversified in Africa and Eurasia, and access from the east and west would have allowed siluroids from these regions to extensively colonize the freshwaters of India. It is likely that <i>Rita rita</i> formed an early part of that colonization, although its nearest relatives among catfishes remain unknown. <i>Rita</i> has been conventionally placed in Bagridae, but the mitogenome analysis refutes that classification.</p><p>An integrative approach to the question of gangesiine origins leads us to consider an &lsquo;Out of Indomalaya&rsquo; dispersalist hypothesis. Given the age of the remaining host groups of Gangesiinae, i.e. between 62 Mya (Bagridae) and 24 Mya (Siluridae), one may hypothesize that Gangesiinae (<i>s.l.</i>, i.e. including <i>Pangasiocestus</i>, <i>Ritacestus</i> and <i>Vermaia</i>) originated in the Indomalayan region during the Palaeogene of the Cenozoic (Fig. 9), thereafter dispersing to Europe and subsequently Africa (Fig. 10), with range expansion and areas of diversification in South and South-East Asia (<i>G. macrones</i>, <i>G. bengalensis</i>, <i>G. \u01b2achai</i> and <i>G</i>. <i>mukutmanipurensis</i>) and in north-east Asia (<i>G. oligonchis</i>, <i>G. parasiluri</i> and <i>G. polyonchis</i>). That Gangesiinae are not a group with deep Gondwanan origins is also supported by the absence of these tapeworms in sub-Saharan Africa, despite schilbeids and bagrids being present there, and in South America. The earliest branching catfish groups, Diplomystidae and Loricariidae of South America, are not hosts of gangesiines or any other early branching proteocephalids. Despite the remarkable diversity of proteocephalids in South American catfishes (de Chambrier <i>et al</i>., 2015, 2017), the foregoing discussion suggests that the Indomalayan region is the original
